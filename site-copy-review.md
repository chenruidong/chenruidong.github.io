## Home View

### Hero

Section id:
cognitive-flexibility

Eyebrow:
Neuroscience of flexible cognition

Intro paragraph:
Primates like humans and monkeys adapt to new situations quickly. The secret is learning generalizable rules from a small number of samples. A monkey can learn to rinse gritty sweet potato by observing another individual. A person can learn to play a new game in a few tries, unlike AI programs. How does the brain implement this cognitive flexibility? We still lack a mechanistic account. My long-term vision is to explain how neural circuits generate flexible behavior by observing, manipulating, and recreating computational elements in the primate brain.

### Research Area 1

Tab label:
Experience and observation

Title:
Evidence integration between self-experience and observation in the cingulate cortex

Paragraph 1:
We asked how observational learning interacts with evidence accumulation in the primate brain. In a two-player task, subjects alternated between acting and observing while both accumulated evidence. Behavior in humans and monkeys revealed a reliable asymmetry: observation is less effective than direct experience even when visual information and motivational state is matched.

Paragraph 2:
Electrophysiology in anterior cingulate cortex showed that experienced and observed evidence are both encoded and projected onto a shared belief axis, with a biased alignment toward self-experience. Population geometry and recurrent neural network modeling suggest this bias emerges from computations within ACC dynamics, providing a mechanistic account of why learning from others is systematically discounted.

### Research Area 2

Tab label:
Song learning and motivation

Title:
Reinforcement learning by intrinsic motivation

Paragraph 1:
Reinforcement learning is one of the most successful algorithmic frameworks discovered in neuroscience. Animals refine action policies through trial and error to maximize reward. But what counts as reward? Is it limited to direct rewards like food and water? In a series of behavioral and neurophysiological experiments in zebra finches, we examined how dopaminergic prediction-error signals support song learning. We found that dopamine neurons encode performance prediction error during singing, and that ventral pallidum is a critical source of these signals.

Paragraph 2:
These findings motivated an actor-critic framework for song learning that links multiple brain regions into a circuit-level account of how intrinsic performance errors drive learning.

### Research Area 3

Tab label:
Multiple motivational drives

Title:
Organization of behavior modules with distinct motivational drives

Paragraph 1:
Biological systems orchestrate multiple goals, such as performing a motor skill and staying close to the flock. How are specialized behavioral modules organized? In zebra finches, singing and locomotion can be studied together, making it possible to compare the motivational drive of distinct behaviors. Our experiments showed that the same broadband noise stimulus can negatively reinforce vocal variants while positively reinforce locomotion, showing multiple intrinsic motivations at work, appropriately routed to distinct behavioral output modules.

Paragraph 2:
Neural analyses from ventral pallidum and dopaminergic midbrain further showed that timing signals can switch between song and movement on sub-second timescales. This supports a view of basal ganglia and midbrain circuits as flexibly gated across behavioral contexts rather than fixed to one function.
